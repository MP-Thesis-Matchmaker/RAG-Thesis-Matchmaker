Themenausschreibung für Masterarbeiten

MA Studierende am IPZ schreiben eine eigenständige Abschlussarbeit (MA Arbeit) als Teil des Master Studienganges. In der Regel finden die Studierenden Thema und Fragestellung alleine und kontaktieren mit einem Vorschlag direkt mögliche Betreuungspersonen.

Ergänzend zu diesem Standardverfahren können Betreuende des IPZ zweimal im Jahr (jeweils Mai und November) Themen ausschreiben, welche im Zusammenhang mit ausgewählten Forschungsschwerpunkten stehen. Studierende, die sich von einer bestimmten Ausschreibung angesprochen fühlen, können sich direkt bei der Kontaktperson melden, um zu besprechen, ob eine MA-Arbeit in dem Bereich möglich wäre.

Ideological Bias in Large Language Models (LLMs)

This project explores a still underdeveloped question of ideological bias in large language models (LLMs). While there is a growing debate in computer science, political science has only begun to consider what it means if AI systems carry implicit worldviews. Too often, attention is directed at specific actors like DeepSeek or Musk’s ventures, while the most influential systems — OpenAI (ChatGPT), Anthropic (Claude), Perplexity — remain insufficiently interrogated.

A central challenge is to unpack what we even mean by “political bias” in this context. The issue is not only about outputs, but also about the raw material (the internet as training data), the cleaning and filtering procedures (what is removed to “protect” models from bias, and at what political cost), and the post-training and alignment layers that strongly shape model behaviour. 

Beyond technical design, political scientists should also ask how such systems are used in practice (research, media, governance, lobbying) and what it means for democratic accountability when information intermediaries become algorithmic.

The dissertation can thus move along two axes:

  1. Theoretical – clarifying what it means to speak of “ideological bias” in AI, and how it compares to concepts of bias in media, institutions, or survey design.
  2. Empirical (optional) – testing different models on survey-like or political prompts, analysing variation across providers, or exploring how alignment and fine-tuning affect ideological orientation.

References:

  • Argyle, L. P., et al. (2023). Out of One, Many: Using Language Models to Simulate Human Samples. Political Analysis.
  • Bisbee, J., et al. (2023). Synthetic Replacements for Human Survey Data? The Perils of Large Language Models. Political Analysis.
  • Boelaert, J., & Ollion, É. (forthcoming). Machine Bias: Generative Large Language Models Have a Worldview of Their Own. Sociological Methods & Research.
  • OpenAI (2024). Defining and Evaluating Political Bias in LLMs.

Abstimmungsforschung in der Schweiz

Kurzbeschrieb: Sind Sie an Abstimmungen in der Schweiz interessiert? Wollen Sie in Ihrer Masterarbeit mit neuen und aktuellen Daten aus Umfragen arbeiten? Mit den Daten des DDS-21 Projekts (https://www.dds21.uzh.ch/de.html) haben Sie eine gute Datenquelle, mit der eine Vielzahl an verschiedenen Fragestellungen bearbeitet werden können.

Stadt/Land-Graben
In den Medien liest man immer wieder von einem extremen Stadt-Land Graben bei Abstimmungen. Beispielsweise die Biodiversitätsinitiative fand grossen Anklang in städtischen Gebieten, während sie wenig Anklang fand bei der Landbevölkerung. Aber was löst diese unterschiedlichen Präferenzen aus? Mit Satellitendaten, die mit unseren Umfragedaten verknüpft worden sind, kann diese Frage auf eine neue Art und Weise beleuchtet werden: Ist es tatsächlich der Wohnort (Stadt oder Dorf) der entscheidend ist für solche Präferenzen, oder ist es viel mehr entscheidend wie viel Natur in meiner Umgebung ist? Dörfler, die im dichtbebauten Dorfkern wohnen sind eher für mehr grün, während auch Städterinnen, die am grünen Stadtrand wohnen nicht für mehr Biodiversität einstehen wollen, da es ja schon vermeintlich viel davon gibt in ihrer Umgebung? Haben die beiden Mietvorlagen auch auf dem Land polarisiert, oder sind diese nur in urbanen Zentren wichtig gewesen?

Was ist affektive Polarisierung in der direkten Demokratie?
Eine grosse Innovation dieses Projekts sind Daten zu affektiver Polarisierung bei Abstimmungen. Bei der Jahrhundertsabstimmung über den Brexit konnte gezeigt werden, dass eine Abstimmung zu tiefen gesellschaftlichen Gräben führen kann, die noch tiefer sind als die Gräben zwischen den Anhänger:innen der verschiedenen Parteien. Was passiert jedoch, wenn wir issuebezogene Affekte und Gruppenidentitäten bei Abstimmungen anschauen in einem Land, in dem regelmässig Abstimmungen stattfinden? Was bedeutet affektive Polarisierung in Bezug auf Abstimmungen überhaupt? Inwiefern unterscheidet sich diese von affektiver Polarisierung bei Parteien? Bei welchen Vorlagen ist die Polarisierung besonders stark und bei welchen schwach?

Wodurch wird affektive Polarisierung bei Vorlagen ausgelöst?
Im Projekt gibt es auch ein Panel mit zwei Wellen. Die erste Welle wurde vor dem Start der Kampagne und die zweite nach dem Abstimmungssonntag durchgeführt. Auf den ersten Blick würde erwartet werden, dass die affektive Polarisierung während der Kampagne stärker wird, da der Wahlkampf Emotionen weckt und harte verbale Schlagabtäusche zwischen Befürworter:innen und Gegner:innen der Vorlagen stattfinden. Erste Auswertungen zeigen jedoch, dass die affektive Polarisierung vor dem Start der Kampagne höher ist, als nach der Abstimmung. Dieser Befund löst verschiedene Fragen aus. Wenn es nicht die Kampagne ist, was löst dann die affektive Polarisierung aus? Was passiert mit den Stimmbürger:innen während der Kampagne? Welche Stimmbürger:innen sind polarisiert und welche nicht?

Wie hängt politische Werbung mit affektiver Polarisierung zusammen?
Wenn die affektive Polarisierung während der Kampagne sinkt, wie hängen politische Werbungen mit affektiver Polarisierung zusammen? In den DDS-21 Daten werden den Befragten auch verschiedene Werbungen gezeigt. Darunter hat es echte Werbungen und erfundene. Danach wird erfragt, welche Emotionen diese Werbungen bei den Stimmberechtigten auslösen. Wie hängt diese emotionale Reaktion auf Werbung mit affektiver Polarisierung zusammen? Sind Stimmberechtigte, die politische Werbung wahrnehmen polarisierter als andere Stimmberechtigte? Wie unterscheiden sich diese Stimmberechtigten von anderen Stimmberechtigten?

Kontakt:
Wenn Sie diese Fragen auch interessant finden und sich vorstellen können, sich mit solchen Fragen bei Ihrer Masterarbeit zu befassen, dann melden Sie sich bei Prof. Dr. Daniel Kübler (daniel.kuebler@ipz.uzh.ch), Dr. Laurent Bernhard (laurent.bernhard@zda.uzh.ch) oder Gabriel Hofmann (hofmann@zda.uzh.ch). Wenn Sie grundsätzlich an Abstimmungsforschung interessiert sind, aber eine andere Fragestellung verfolgen möchten finden Sie diese Daten auf SWISSUbase (https://www.swissubase.ch/en/catalogue/studies/20715/20823/datasets). Dort können Sie sich durch das Codebook wühlen und sich eine eigene Fragestellung überlegen. Weitere Informationen und Inspirationen finden Sie auch auf der Projektwebsite.

Mapping Informal Power: Measuring Reputation-based Success of Swiss MPs

Political reputation has traditionally been measured through surveys asking MPs or staffers to name the most influential figures in parliament. More recently, social network analysis—using data such as cosponsorships, speech citations, or seating proximity—has offered new insights into informal influence, helping identify MPs who act as connectors, agenda-setters, or brokers across factions. Reputation, however, is not only individual. MPs often operate within parliamentary groups or caucuses that pursue shared policy goals. Success and influence must therefore also be assessed at the group level. For instance, the Green-Liberal group was instrumental in advancing the 2013 marriage equality initiative, which passed in 2020, survived a referendum in 2021, and was enacted in 2023—an achievement reflecting the group’s collective efforts rather than any single MP. 

This thesis can explore how reputation-based success is captured through network data, how group-level dynamics shape influence, and how reputational impact unfolds in major legislative cases. It can also compare survey-based and network-based approaches to measuring political reputation.

Kontakt: Sophia Schlosser

Classifying Speaking Styles in Parliamentary Speech

Political speeches often reflect distinct speaking styles that shape how messages are received by audiences. While linguists have long studied rhetorical speaking styles, political science has yet to fully engage with this dimension of political communication. Yet, speaking style can significantly influence not only how a speech is perceived, but also how consistently used styles affect perceptions of the speaker.

This Master's thesis is both theoretical and methodological in nature. First, it develops a systematic classification of speaking styles, such as narrator, simplifier, rhetorical speaker, and analyst, drawing on established frameworks from linguistics. Second, it proposes and implements an operationalization strategy to identify and measure these styles in Swiss parliamentary speeches.

Potential research questions include: Do Members of Parliament (MPs) exhibit consistent speaking styles? How do speaking styles vary across policy topics? And can certain speaking styles increase the likelihood of persuading or swaying votes?

Kontakt:  Laurence Brandenberger

Political Polarization and Anti-establishment Rhetoric

Anti-establishment feelings and political polarisation are some of the most important concepts used to understand the behaviour of parties and politicians and their interactions with multiple audiences. Despite a rich theoretical literature discussing anti-system feelings, such as populism, technocracy, and polarization, we do not know how these interact.  Our dataset of national European parliaments with anti-establishment speeches classified into technocracy and populism enables this research. This opens the door to further understanding of how polarisation plays a role in the use of those rhetoric types. 

This is a master thesis topic under the chair of Political Methodology, Prof. Marco Steenbergen.

Requirement: MA students with strong programming skills (python or R), interested in political polarisation, rhetoric and text analysis. 

Kontakt: Marco Steenbergen

Ideology and Rhetoric

Anti-establishment feelings are some of the most important concepts used to understand the behaviour of parties and politicians, as well as their interactions with multiple audiences. A rich theoretical literature discussing anti-system feelings, such as populism and technocracy, shows how intricate their relationship is to the left and right ideologies, yet we are missing a systematic way of measuring anti-establishment feelings independent of ideology. This MA thesis builds upon our dataset of national European parliaments with anti-establishment speeches classified into technocracy and populism and builds a method to remove ideology from the existing classifier.

This is a master thesis topic under the chair of Political Methodology, Prof. Marco Steenbergen. 

Requirement: MA students with strong programming skills (python) and text analysis knowledge.

Large Language Models for Political Party Positions

Knowing where political parties stand is of great importance in the study of party strategy, electoral competition, and representation. Until now, data on party positions have come primarily from party manifestos and from expert surveys. However, there is another data source that has gone under-utilized: political speeches. In this project, the goal is to assess how ideological and issue positions can best be gleaned from political speech acts using large language models. The goal is to perform a test run for the United Kingdom, where speeches are readily available in a digitized format.

Measuring Political Ideology

Measuring Political Ideology and Social Influence through Cosponsorship Behavior

Political scientists have traditionally relied on roll-call voting data to infer the ideological positions (i.e., ideal points, political stances) of members of parliament (MPs). But what happens when roll-call voting data is unavailable? An alternative approach uses cosponsorship data—where MPs support bills by adding their signatures—to uncover patterns of ideological alignment and social influence.

The aim of this thesis is to analyze cosponsorship behavior among MPs as an indicator of their ideological positions, party loyalty, and social dynamics. Using Item Response Theory (IRT) models, the thesis seeks to develop robust proxies for political ideology, alliance formation strategies, and party cohesion.

Data: This thesis is part of the DemocraSci Project, which leverages a graph relational database capturing all parliamentary activities in the Swiss National Parliament from 1891 to the present.

Methodology: The thesis will apply IRT models to scale cosponsorship behavior, providing innovative insights into MP interactions in the Swiss political landscape.

This thesis is ideal for students with a strong interest in political methodology and a passion for exploring new techniques to measure latent concepts in political science.

Kontakt: Laurence Brandenberger

Signals of Influence

Signals of Influence: Unpacking Cosponsorship and Bill Passage in Switzerland

The focus of this thesis is on cosponsorship relations among Members of Parliament (MPs) and their relationship with the passage of bills over time.

Cosponsorship is often viewed as a strategic exchange (tit-for-tat) in legislative research, but it also serves as a signal of support or influence. Preliminary insights suggest that these signaling dynamics can correlate with the success of specific types of bills (particularly in the context of postulates), providing a compelling lens through which to understand legislative processes.

Data: This thesis is part of the DemocraSci Project, which leverages a graph relational database capturing all parliamentary activities in the Swiss National Parliament from 1891 to the present.

Methodology: The thesis analyzes the evolution of cosponsorship networks in the Swiss parliament and investigates how collaborative diversity influences bill passage.

This thesis is ideal for students with an interest in understanding political networks, quantitative analysis, and Swiss (historical) legislative behavior.

Kontakt: Laurence Brandenberger

Decoding Migration Debates

Decoding Migration Debates: A Semi-Automated Analysis of Swiss Parliamentary Texts 

Migration has become a central issue in Swiss politics since the mid-20th century, with a significant increase in migration-related parliamentary initiatives. These bills address diverse topics such as asylum law, deportation, border control, and integration policies, reflecting societal changes to address political challenges. Legislative activity often coincides with migration waves from countries such as Hungary, Kosovo, and Ukraine, while popular initiatives have fueled debates on immigration control and societal integration. 

This master's thesis analyzes the evolution of migration-related issues in Swiss parliamentary debates over time, focusing on shifts in thematic priorities, the legislative role of migration, and the development of partisan dynamics. 

Data: This thesis is part of the DemocraSci Project, which leverages a graph relational database capturing all parliamentary activities in the Swiss National Parliament from 1891 to the present. 

Methodology: The thesis employs a semi-automated dictionary-based approach to content analysis to systematically analyze large-scale parliamentary text corpora. 

This thesis is ideal for students with an interest in quantitative analysis and in understanding the historical evolution of Switzerland's legislative focus on migration issues.

Kontakt: Laurence Brandenberger

Social Media, Echo Chambers & Epistemic Bubbles

Echo chambers are online communication environments in which individuals encounter only beliefs or opinions that coincide with their own. Given their potential harm to democracy - such as political polarization and segregation -, it is essential that we investigate questions such as: How do echo chambers develop online? Are dissenting voices accidentally left out or actively discredited? Does exposure to dissenting voices reinforce or shatter echo chambers? These questions are important as they explain the causes, consequences and ways to deal with echo chambers, and can be investigated by identifying relevant case studies, collecting Twitter data, and employing Social Network Analysis (SNA).

Is the referendum really a damocles sword?

Many theories of how direct democracy affects political outcomes are based on the idea that decision makers will compromise to avoid a referendum campaign. Neidhardt (1970) is an early example and described the optional referendum hanging like a sword of Damocles over parliamentary decision making. If legislators do not compromise and exclude a large enough group, they can quickly find themselves in a referendum campaign and potentially lose. To avoid this, it may be better to make a small compromise and avoid the "gamble" of a direct democratic vote.

Some theories find (indirect) evidence for this mechanism (e.g., Leemann & Wasserfallen, 2016), others do not (Lascher et al. 1996). Since there are some exogenous changes in signature thresholds, one can estimate the causal effect of the referendum on MPs' probability of compromise.

Keywords: direct democracy, Switzerland, causal inference, optional referendum.

Political Parties and the EU

What are the positions of political parties in Switzerland and Europe on the EU issue? How do different media outlets report on these positions? How have these positions evolved over time? And how do developments around Brexit influence both media coverage and party positions? These and other questions can be investigated with an extensive media dataset (news articles and metadata from a dozen European countries).

Euroscepticism and the future of Swiss-EU relations

Why do some citizens support European integration while others oppose it? To what extent can political parties manufacture support and opposition for the EU? What’s the relationship between Euroscepticism and populism? In light of recent events, what lessons have Swiss citizens drawn from Brexit? Did the pandemic increase people’s support for international cooperation? And what’s the future of Swiss-EU relations after the end of the negotiations on the Institutional Framework Agreement? These and other questions can be investigated using available, anonymised individual data from a four-wave panel survey field ed in Switzerland between 2019 and 2021.

Diesen und ähnlichen Fragen kann man mit vorhandenen, anonymisierten Individualdaten aus Deutschland, UK, Schweden und Spanien.
